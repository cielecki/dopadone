Symptom

Fear and avoidance

You delay, dodge, rationalise — anything to not feel the discomfort. The more you fight the fear, the bigger it grows.

Courage isn’t the absence of fear — it’s acting alongside it. The goal isn’t to remove discomfort but to learn to sit with it. Below: accepting fear instead of fighting it, a ready calming kit, and training courage on small things.

This page isn't typically flagged for the selected profile — shown because you opened it directly.

Methods that help

  • Talk back to your brain with proof (challenge the thought)

    When pre-task anxiety or an 'I'm the worst' spiral hits, notice it happening and answer your brain with concrete proof from the past ('I gave a talk last week and it went OK'). Interrogate the thought like a lawyer for the other side and write out the worst case.

    B · good 2 sources

  • Train courage on small things

    Practise micro-discomfort where the stakes are zero: 'I asked for three, not four', send a dish back at a restaurant.

    B · good 2 sources

  • Feel the fear and do it (acceptance)

    Don't fight the fear (that grows it) and don't flee — board with it, expecting it. Sit the fear in the seat beside you.

    B · good 1 source

  • Habituate to orgasm with a partner (graded exposure)

    If you orgasm alone but not with a partner — masturbate while the partner kisses you or whispers in your ear. A reliable orgasm + the partner's presence = the pressure barrier fades over time.

    B · good 1 source

  • Scan the body and release tension (break the stress loop)

    When you feel stressed, actively find the most tense muscle (jaw, shoulders, neck, belly) and consciously 'let it go'. Relaxing the body starves the feedback loop and stress drops — without fighting your thoughts.

    B · good

  • Distinguish stress, anxiety and depression

    They differ by control and trajectory: stress resolves when its situation resolves; anxiety is a state you can't switch off (you'd find new stressors even if current ones vanished); depression is the point beyond, where you've given up and see no light at the end of the tunnel. Knowing which you're in points to the right response.

    C · weak / preliminary 3 sources

  • Active breathwork to empty an overactive mind

    For a mind too busy to sit still, force the energy out first: ~30 strong, forceful exhalations (bellows breath / kapalbhati) exhaust the conscious mind, then rest fully in the calm of the breath that follows. A sensation-rich anchor beats plain breath-watching.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Alcohol is a poor fit for the ADHD brain

    Alcohol binds the GABA receptor for an instant calming, social-confidence hit the ADHD brain leans into — but the next-day rebound is jangly, heart-racing and anxious, and rapid intoxication plus impulsivity drives drinking more. For social anxiety, breathe into the moment and focus on listening instead.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Demand-avoidance strategies

    When the mere 'I must' triggers resistance — reframe the task and remove the sense of compulsion.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • A low-information diet

    Deliberately restrict inbound information: unsubscribe from social-media notifications, skip routine news, adopt a no-clickbait policy, and pull updates on a topic only occasionally when you choose to. Constant intake clutters the mind, and the brain's negativity bias turns that clutter into anxiety.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Name medical distress as sensory, and ask for accommodations

    Panic in clinics, dentists and during procedures can be a sensory and neurological reaction, not a phobia or a personality flaw. Reframing it that way changes your self-understanding and lets you tell providers concretely what they can do: expect different communication, a possible shutdown, very high anxiety, and trouble putting things into words.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Regulate emotion early — change the situation, not the feeling

    In ADHD, suppressing an emotion once it has peaked is nearly impossible, so intervene early in the chain. Inventory the situations that reliably set you off and avoid or opt out of them; if you're already in one, modify it or redeploy your attention away (look away, distract, daydream) instead of trying to white-knuckle the feeling down.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Send a standing access rider before you start working together

    Instead of asking for accommodations case-by-case as problems arise, write one standing document — an access rider or 'manual of me' — that lists your communication needs and preferences, and send it the first time a collaborator wants to work with you. State needs once, up front, and make it collaborative by inviting theirs too.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Sensate focus: a slow restart of intimacy

    After a long sexless stretch (or with strong anxiety), the 'sensate focus' program is used — a slow, graded reintroduction of touch and sex. The spark can return, but the realistic horizon is about 6–9 months.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Social-anxiety reframe: you're an NPC in someone else's game

    Nobody is watching you because everyone is absorbed in themselves — you're a 'non-playable character' (NPC) in their game. This frees you from the paralyzing belief that everyone at the party is judging you.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • A fear kit in your back pocket

    Prepare a tool list in advance. Just having them 'in your back pocket' lowers tension — even if you don't use them.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Use a fixed daily routine as pre-solved time management

    For an autistic brain that finds time estimation effortful and anxiety-provoking, a repeated daily routine does the time management for you. When wake, drop-off, coffee and commute happen at the same times every day, there are no calculations left to make — the 'how long will this take' load disappears, freeing capacity for genuinely new events.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • A four-step plan for a hard disclosure

    Turn coming out as autistic into a small, deliberate plan: write a one-or-two-sentence script leading with the most important line, pick a moment with positive energy and space to absorb it, prepare for the realistic response (not the best case), and then just do it. Say your few phrases and stop — wait for their response instead of over-explaining.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Agree a physical exit signal with your companion

    Social burnout can hit a freeze where you know you need to leave but can't produce the words to explain it, so you stay trapped. Agree a physical signal in advance — e.g. three taps on the arm — that tells your companion to invent the excuse and get you both out, so escape doesn't depend on you speaking.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • An activity date instead of a face-to-face dinner

    For a neurodivergent person a better first date is a shared activity (mini golf, walk, gallery) rather than a dinner — the activity removes the pressure of eye contact and the question-after-question 'interview'. Best preceded by a phone/FaceTime call.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • An exit plan and your own ride home

    Decide before you arrive exactly when and how you'll get home, and keep control of your own departure (drive yourself, even separately from a partner). The certainty alone tames anxiety so you can actually be present — and the 'Irish goodbye' plus a follow-up text short-circuits the rejection spiral.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Buffer for the impossible standard (and safe people's flexibility)

    If you've internalised a 'flake / let-down' identity, even a delay outside your control can trigger a trauma response (meltdown, 'I've ruined it'). Two moves: (1) notice that safe people offer FAR more flexibility than your impossible standard; (2) over-buffer the situations you fail most — leave two trains earlier.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Deep-pressure sensory play to down-regulate

    Press your hands hard into resistive, mouldable material — kinetic sand, soft 'cloud' putty, a squishable tray. The hand pressure feeds calming proprioceptive input, which can de-escalate over-arousal on demand. It's not just for kids, and it doesn't have to be expensive.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Delay every invitation, then default to no

    Never answer an invitation or time-demand in the moment. Say you'll check your diary and reply within a couple of days, hide your phone, and use the window to run a written pros-and-cons list. Default to declining or delaying rather than accepting — it's far easier to upgrade a no to a yes than to downgrade a yes to a no.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Do a lap before you talk to anyone

    On arrival, walk one full lap of the space before initiating any interaction — learn the layout, the vibe, where the snacks, restrooms and exits are. Mapping the room first builds known fallback spots that lower anxiety before the social demand begins.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Give yourself a role at the event

    Take on a concrete role — host, dish-washer, the one who welcomes newcomers, the parent on duty. A defined role supplies a script and a purpose that replace open-ended, ambiguous social demands with structured, predictable behaviour.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Label a thought as just a thought

    The mind generates a constant automatic stream of comments, and most of them aren't worth trusting. When a thought appears, name it as a thought rather than a fact, then choose whether it deserves to be acted on — instead of automatically believing every stupid thing that pops into your head.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Name the emotion behind the task, not just the task

    Procrastination is usually emotion-avoidance, not a willpower failure. Before starting, name the feeling the task triggers (fear, boredom, overwhelm) so the avoidance becomes conscious and the task feels less aversive — and catch the shame about not-yet-starting early, before it spirals into more avoidance.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Outsource logistics and boundary-enforcement to someone else

    ADHD means you won't remember to ask for the details until it's too late, and an excitable people-pleasing brain caves on boundaries 'just this once'. Hand both jobs to a third party: have someone gather event logistics on your behalf via an automated reminder, and let them decline offers that break your rules — they aren't subject to your in-the-moment impulses.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Plan a social event like you'd plan a work talk

    Treat a social event as a planned event you take seriously: research who'll be there, prep a few light topics, sleep and eat beforehand. Offloading the work to BEFORE the event removes the in-the-moment cognitive load a neurodivergent brain can't reliably improvise.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Play the numbers to blunt rejection

    Over-focusing on one thing you might be rejected for amplifies RSD. Spread the risk instead: apply broadly, expect roughly a one-in-ten hit rate, and plan for rejection as the default. When any single 'no' is expected statistical noise rather than a personal verdict, it stops mattering so much.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Prepare bullet points, not a script, for talks

    A rigid full script fights the ADHD tendency to follow tangents, so it gets abandoned mid-talk. Prepare only three or four rough bullet points as anchors and improvise the connective material — and keep a mental library of pre-tested 'Lego brick' anecdotes and metaphors to drop in any order and land on when a tangent leaves you lost.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Ride out a compulsion instead of checking

    When the urge to perform a compulsion (like checking) hits, don't give in. The anxiety will spike at first, but the longer you hold without acting, the more it falls on its own. Tolerating the rising anxiety and letting it crest is how the loop weakens instead of being fed.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Run an obsessive thought through the ANT questions

    For sticky obsessive or automatic negative thoughts, write the thought down and interrogate it: Is it true? Is it absolutely true? How do I feel when I have this thought? Then deliberately turn it to the opposite. Treat this thought-challenging as a daily practice, not an occasional fix.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Send the short honest text before avoidance compounds

    Leaving a party without goodbyes or forgetting to reply feels too awkward to fix — and the awkwardness only grows the longer you avoid it, until a day becomes weeks and a friendship quietly ends. Bite the bullet and send the short honest message immediately: 'sorry I had to go, wasn't feeling great, lovely night.' Reversed, 99% of people react with 'no worries.'

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Serotonin-support supplements for worry

    For chronic worry or obsessive tendencies, some natural supplements (saffron, 5-HTP) are proposed to support serotonin availability in the brain. If you try saffron, look for a standardized, clinically-studied extract rather than generic 'saffron', since the mood-active compounds vary widely between products. Evidence is preliminary and supplements are an adjunct, not a replacement for proper care.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Sit with money stress instead of shutting down

    Money instability flips the brain out of problem-solving and into survival mode. Staying present with the discomfort keeps the thinking brain online — and addressing the underlying insecurity beats using money to prop up identity.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Deep pressure calms the nervous system

    Firm, sustained pressure through the hands and body (squeezing, squishing, pressing into resistive material) feeds proprioceptive input that nudges the body toward the calming, parasympathetic side — a bottom-up way to down-regulate that doesn't need words or willpower.
