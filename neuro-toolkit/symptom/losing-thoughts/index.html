Symptom

Losing thoughts / interrupting

You're afraid you'll forget, so you cut others off; an uncaptured thought 'eats' your working memory for a week.

Behind interrupting and distraction often sits weaker working memory — the fear of losing a thought. The fix isn’t ‘try harder’ but to externalise: write it down instead of holding it in your head.

This page isn't typically flagged for the selected profile — shown because you opened it directly.

Methods that help

  • Mindfulness in movement (and 20 star jumps on a bad day)

    The two best-evidenced ADHD interventions are mindfulness and exercise. Still mindfulness is very hard with ADHD — do it in active form (mindful running, dancing, music). Even 20 star jumps on a bad-weather day shifts the whole day's trajectory.

    A · strong evidence 1 source

  • The 2-minute rule + one inbox

    If it takes ≤2 min — do it now. Everything else goes into ONE place (notebook/card), or the thought 'eats' your working memory for a week.

    B · good 1 source

  • You don't have to read the whole book (close the unhelpful story)

    When your brain spins a distressing narrative (e.g. rumination before sleep), you don't have to finish it. Notice it as 'a story you're telling yourself', ask one question — 'is this story helping me?' — and if not, deliberately drop it. You're the author of this book, so you can close it halfway.

    B · good 1 source

  • Active breathwork to empty an overactive mind

    For a mind too busy to sit still, force the energy out first: ~30 strong, forceful exhalations (bellows breath / kapalbhati) exhaust the conscious mind, then rest fully in the calm of the breath that follows. A sensation-rich anchor beats plain breath-watching.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Emotional bookmarks for a weak working memory

    ADHD brains store emotional memories far better than neutral facts, so attach a vivid felt consequence to a task to make it stick — and run the reminder as a calm, kind self-instruction plus a timer, not the usual hostile 'angry neighbour' inner voice.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Externalize it — put the information, the time, and the problem in front of you

    ADHD doesn't reliably guide behaviour from internal mental representations, so move them outside: write what you must do and keep it physically in your visual field, make time physical with a visible timer/counter, and when mental problem-solving stalls, take the problem into your hands with movable objects.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • A low-information diet

    Deliberately restrict inbound information: unsubscribe from social-media notifications, skip routine news, adopt a no-clickbait policy, and pull updates on a topic only occasionally when you choose to. Constant intake clutters the mind, and the brain's negativity bias turns that clutter into anxiety.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Aim for neutrality, not happiness

    In depression you don't crave happiness — it feels as far away as Mars; what you actually want is boredom and neutrality, even seconds of not thinking about your own mind. Recovery comes from noticing one minute of non-rumination and trying to extend it, not from chasing joy.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Compensate for aphantasia with words and routine

    If you can't picture things in your mind, route around the missing visual channel. Encode directions as a short verbal sequence of landmarks, train focused listening into a verbatim auditory memory, and use fixed routes and routine to build a familiarity scaffold instead of recognising places and people by sight.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Creatine for the brain, not just muscle

    Creatine — familiar as a strength supplement — also supports brain energy metabolism, with reported benefits for memory, mood and cognition, including when you're sleep deprived. For neurodivergent people with disrupted sleep, it's a low-cost lever worth considering, with usual caveats about checking supplements with a professional.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Decide by your values, and split the two phases

    Stop weighing every pro and con — that drives an endless hunt for more information. Instead, judge only the options that matter most against your own values. And separate decisions into two phases: generate ideas freely first (divergent), then narrow to one (convergent) — don't do both at once.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Here-and-now anchor (Barkley's 4 questions)

    When you lose your sense of self and scatter, use a simple mindfulness based on Russell Barkley's executive-function model: who am I, where am I, what am I doing, how long have I got.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Label a thought as just a thought

    The mind generates a constant automatic stream of comments, and most of them aren't worth trusting. When a thought appears, name it as a thought rather than a fact, then choose whether it deserves to be acted on — instead of automatically believing every stupid thing that pops into your head.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Reframe an RSD spiral with a four-step evidence check

    An RSD spiral can't be argued out of the feeling, but a short structured step buys a breather to climb out. Name the thought you're believing, list what happens if you believe it, state the opposite thought and its consequences, then weigh the actual evidence for each — usually thin for the negative, ample for the positive.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Rule of threes (max three categories)

    Don't overwhelm working memory with more than three categories. Tidying: 'keep / throw / not sure'. Clothes: three baskets (wash / re-wear / put away). Ideas: 'now / soon / later'.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Run an obsessive thought through the ANT questions

    For sticky obsessive or automatic negative thoughts, write the thought down and interrogate it: Is it true? Is it absolutely true? How do I feel when I have this thought? Then deliberately turn it to the opposite. Treat this thought-challenging as a daily practice, not an occasional fix.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Serotonin-support supplements for worry

    For chronic worry or obsessive tendencies, some natural supplements (saffron, 5-HTP) are proposed to support serotonin availability in the brain. If you try saffron, look for a standardized, clinically-studied extract rather than generic 'saffron', since the mood-active compounds vary widely between products. Evidence is preliminary and supplements are an adjunct, not a replacement for proper care.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Solve overwhelm by subtracting, not adding

    When overwhelmed, the instinct is to ADD a fix — a tool, a habit, more 'self-care'. But true rest needs you to actively REMOVE things from your baseline, because so much daily activity runs on autopilot that 'doing nothing' just preserves the load. Before adding anything, ask what you could take away.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Train the brain with a fast racket sport

    Fast, strategic racket sports load several brain regions at once — the cerebellum (coordination and processing speed), the parietal lobes (tracking the ball in space) and the frontal lobes (constant strategy). Table tennis is a strong pick because it's fast and strategic; get a coach and actually get good, since the skill-building is what does the work.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Write it down instead of interrupting

    While someone speaks to the end, WRITE DOWN your questions instead of cutting in. Half will turn out moot — the speaker answers them anyway.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Find the function of the emotion, then name it

    Instead of treating emotions as enemies, ask what each one is protecting you from (e.g. resentment held you back from shouting at your boss). Pair it with 'name it to tame it': labelling the feeling (a feelings wheel helps) calms the amygdala.

    D · none / theory 4 sources

  • Steer the attention drift, don't fight it

    When focus slips during sex, don't force yourself to 'stay present' — deliberately steer the drift toward arousing things (a hot memory, a fantasy). Then the drift doesn't kill the pleasure and you can still finish.

    D · none / theory 1 source

  • Use mental models as decision filters

    Pre-built mental models and decision procedures act as external filters that interrupt impulsive decisions and reduce spiralling — instead of trying to suppress an impulse with willpower, give the brain a concrete process to follow, so the pause becomes structural rather than effortful.

    D · none / theory 1 source

Why this happens

Usually several mechanisms stack at once. Click to understand which one is yours.

  • Weak working memory

    A thought you don't write down vanishes — and the brain 'guards' it at the cost of attention, driving interrupting and distraction.

  • Tidal dopamine and noradrenaline (not low, dysregulated)

    In ADHD dopamine and noradrenaline aren't simply low — they're dysregulated like a tide: sometimes out (scattered, unfocused, flat), sometimes rushing in (hyperfocus, hyper-energy). How you are in half an hour can be totally different.

  • The brain fixes problems by adding, not subtracting

    Faced with a problem, people overwhelmingly reach for something to ADD — a new tool, habit, app, commitment — and rarely consider removing something, even when subtraction is the simpler fix. This default quietly piles load onto an already-overloaded system.

  • Aphantasia (no, or faint, mental imagery)

    Some neurodivergent people have little or no voluntary mental imagery — 'visualise an apple' returns nothing, or a wispy image that fades under attention. It's a spectrum, often co-occurs with face-blindness, and reroutes how memory, navigation and learning actually work.
