Symptom

I feel broken and worthless

You beat yourself up, feel never-enough, carry impostor syndrome, and can't accept or like yourself.

Shame and ‘I’m useless’ grow from years of un-affirming messages, not facts. Below — a counter-evidence journal against impostor syndrome, and curiosity instead of self-judgment.

This page isn't typically flagged for the selected profile — shown because you opened it directly.

Methods that help

  • Evidence & wins journal (against imposter syndrome)

    After each feared event, journal the evidence it went well; also keep a daily wins journal — the ADHD brain forgets successes fast, so confidence never accumulates.

    B · good 6 sources

  • Talk back to your brain with proof (challenge the thought)

    When pre-task anxiety or an 'I'm the worst' spiral hits, notice it happening and answer your brain with concrete proof from the past ('I gave a talk last week and it went OK'). Interrogate the thought like a lawyer for the other side and write out the worst case.

    B · good 2 sources

  • A morning letter to yourself

    In your worst mental-health stretches, write yourself a short letter each morning ('you are not a failure, you've done your hard things') — externalized self-compassion counters the shame loop.

    B · good 1 source

  • Reach the root, not the band-aid

    Don't stop at 'I shouldn't take it to heart' — that's a band-aid. Ask the deeper question: why does THIS resonate with me when I've let other things go? Tracing why a specific jab lands reaches the root insecurity instead of patching the reaction.

    B · good 1 source

  • Inner-child work (letters, mirror, visualization)

    Build a relationship with your inner child through concrete, repeated exercises: two-way letters (adult↔child), mirror work, visualization, movement, self-portraits. Write the letters by hand. Do it autonomously, alone — don't make healing dependent on a partner.

    C · weak / preliminary 4 sources

  • The enjoyment test: laziness or overwhelm

    What looks like laziness is usually overwhelm. The test: laziness would be a choice and would feel good; overwhelm is lying there with NO enjoyment while a merciless inner critic tears you up. And the shame of 'only I can't cope' disarms in daylight — in a group of other ADHD adults.

    C · weak / preliminary 4 sources

  • Unmasking in small steps

    There is no sudden unmasking — do it gradually and safely. First map your situation (family, friends), pick one trusted person and slowly reveal yourself ('unpeeling'). A useful start: list things you constantly do against yourself, and things you struggle with yet still do well.

    C · weak / preliminary 4 sources

  • Engineer your thrive-conditions (instead of 'try harder')

    'Try harder' is wrong because effort isn't the missing variable — people with ADHD already 'run twice as hard to get half as far'. ADHD isn't a lack of effort; it's effort poured into systems designed for a non-ADHD brain. Instead of escalating effort, identify the conditions in which you thrive (time of day, visual timer, body doubling) and deliberately engineer as many of them as possible.

    C · weak / preliminary 3 sources

  • Listen to the first reflex (find yourself under the mask)

    To tell your real self from the mask, notice your knee-jerk emotional reactions. A sudden, spontaneous surge of excitement is your inner child signalling what you actually care about — before the 'should' kicks in.

    C · weak / preliminary 3 sources

  • Low-maintenance friendship

    The most durable friendship model for neurodivergent people is one with no pressure to text daily — you can reconnect once a month (or after a year) and pick up like 10 minutes passed, with no flood of messages, shame or guilt.

    C · weak / preliminary 3 sources

  • Ask 'but why?' down to the root

    Sit with yourself and keep asking 'but why?' like a child until you reach the real root motive — are you acting from self-love or from fear and low self-esteem.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Connection before correction (curiosity, not criticism)

    Before you change a child's behaviour, tend the relationship — correction without connection won't work. Be curious not critical: ask 'what were you thinking before you did that?' instead of assuming a motive. And say it outright: 'I'm in your corner, no matter what'.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Don't hold an ADHD child back a grade — get accommodations instead

    Grade retention is a failed intervention repeated: it doesn't fix the underlying ADHD and instead causes lost motivation, more aggression or depression, worse peer relationships and a much higher dropout rate. Provide special-education support and accommodations early — cohorts that get them drop out at roughly the typical rate.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Look within for the emotional weak spot, not the trigger

    When a reaction is disproportionate and recurs across many different situations and people, the common factor is you — a carried wound, not the environment. Name your recurring 'critical-hit' themes, watch for them, and trace each back to where it formed.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Match the job to the brain

    Putting an ADHD person in a mundane, easygoing admin role is a recipe for disaster; complex, difficult, enthralling work is where they excel. Treat finding the right-fit environment as a first-line intervention — it can even reduce the need for medication.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Resolve the team friction before grinding harder

    Unresolved frustration with the people you work or play with blocks flow state — and flow is what actually wins. Talking through feelings with each other to reduce blame improves performance more than extra hours; full effort that fails also stings worse than half-effort, so make it safe to try.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Size the task to the executive age, not the calendar age

    In ADHD the 'executive age' — the age of self-regulation — runs roughly 30% behind chronological age, so demands sized to the real age routinely overshoot capacity. Right-size expectations, task length and supervision to the younger executive age, and break long tasks into small quotas that fit it.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Treat ADHD as a chronic condition — stay on treatment

    ADHD medication manages symptoms while taken but doesn't cure the disorder, so stopping returns you to baseline — like a diabetic stopping insulin. Plan treatment as ongoing for as long as impairment lasts, not a time-limited childhood course, and weigh that within-person, on-med months reduce serious life risks, not just symptoms.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Admit it and forgive yourself anyway

    To get unstuck after a failure, admit you messed up and then forgive yourself regardless. If dropping the ball is allowed, you're free to pick it up again; paranoia about ever dropping it is exactly what stops you from starting.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Check the comparison, not just the criticism

    Ego is anything that compares you to another person — and flattery that ranks you above someone else is ego too, a hidden source of suffering despite feeling good. Treat comparative praise with the same caution as criticism, and let awareness step in after an automatic reaction to defuse it.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Check the fulfilment, not just the metrics

    Repeatedly doubling down on whatever others react to turns you into a 'caricature of yourself' and drives burnout — you end up making things you aren't fulfilled by. And you can't satisfy everyone's contradictory feedback anyway, so check whether the work still fulfills you, not just whether it performs.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Design for your worst week, with a return path built in

    Build your systems around your failures, not your successes: a routine that only works on a good week collapses on a bad one. Design for the low-capacity week first, and bake in a restart protocol so a single missed day doesn't snowball into months of abandonment.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Find a neuro-affirming professional (validation itself heals)

    In a crisis, look for a professional who is neuro-affirming and understands how neurodivergence affects mental health. Just hearing 'there is a real cause, you're not broken or imagining it' is therapeutic. After diagnosis, drive your own psychoeducation — who can help, where to find support, what to read.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Find your tribe (the more niche, the surer the hit)

    To find your people, recall what you loved as a child (before being told it wasn't appropriate), pick something niche — rock choir, pottery, running club, trampolining — and just go. The more niche the activity, the more reliably you'll meet someone from your tribe.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Hold onto neuroplasticity: this isn't permanent

    Your brain physically rewires its structure with every interaction and place, so the depressed/addicted person you are now is not a fixed identity — a different version of you will eventually show up. Two framings that carry it: depression is weather and you are the sky; a breakdown is breaking open the radio to see how it works.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Hold the strengths and the struggle together

    The hard parts of neurodivergence and its real strengths — pattern recognition, creative problem-solving, hyperfocus, the spark — are both true at the same time, and neither cancels the other. The reframe is to keep the whole picture rather than collapsing into a pure-deficit or pure-superpower story.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • It's the system, not your willpower

    Repeated productivity failures with ADHD are a systems-and-information gap, not a character flaw — the tools failed because they were built for a neurotypical brain that supplies discipline and consistency on demand. Treat the struggle as a fit problem and build a low-willpower system around how your brain actually works.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Judge symptoms by frequency and impairment, not just recognition

    A few ADHD traits ringing true isn't a diagnosis. The criteria require crossing a threshold (6 of 9 symptoms in children, 5 in adults), each rated as happening 'often', present for six months, AND impairing major life activities. A trait becomes a disorder at the frequent, severe, impairing extreme — and 'it's just a spectrum' doesn't make it less real.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Name the emotion behind the task, not just the task

    Procrastination is usually emotion-avoidance, not a willpower failure. Before starting, name the feeling the task triggers (fear, boredom, overwhelm) so the avoidance becomes conscious and the task feels less aversive — and catch the shame about not-yet-starting early, before it spirals into more avoidance.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Name your inner critic as a character

    Externalize the inner critic as a named character (e.g. 'Critical Kelvin'), recognize it formed as a protective coping mechanism, then thank it and gently disagree — 'I know you sound like a jerk, but you're trying to keep me safe; thank you, but I think you're wrong'. Treat it with kindness instead of fighting or silencing it.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Practice, not discipline — and treat self-care as an experiment

    You'll never become 'disciplined' with an ADHD brain, so stop fighting impulse with willpower. Build habits by practice (each attempt is a rep, expect to forget), and do a recovery activity as an experiment to OBSERVE your guilt rather than to 'fix' yourself.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Read symptoms as brain signals, not character flaws

    Distractibility, disorganization and impulsivity aren't character flaws — they're signs of how the brain is functioning. Reading them that way drops the self-blame and pivots you toward strategies and, where it applies, treatment, instead of self-criticism. Untreated genuine ADHD compounds over time, so it's worth addressing rather than leaving alone.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Run an obsessive thought through the ANT questions

    For sticky obsessive or automatic negative thoughts, write the thought down and interrogate it: Is it true? Is it absolutely true? How do I feel when I have this thought? Then deliberately turn it to the opposite. Treat this thought-challenging as a daily practice, not an occasional fix.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Separate fault from powerlessness

    We self-blame for things we feel powerless over — but fault and powerlessness can't coexist. If it's truly your fault you had power; if you're powerless it isn't your fault. Owning only the part you can actually control restores agency.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Sit with money stress instead of shutting down

    Money instability flips the brain out of problem-solving and into survival mode. Staying present with the discomfort keeps the thinking brain online — and addressing the underlying insecurity beats using money to prop up identity.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Speak about yourself kindly (and 'the old me')

    Against chronic self-criticism: speak to yourself kindly (as you would to a friend) and frame old self-critical traits as 'that was the old me'. Caveat from the research: forced grand affirmations ('I'm wonderful') can backfire for people with low self-esteem — what works is kindness and realism, not positive slogans by force.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Spend your organization points where they matter

    Treat executive/organization energy as a finite pool of points and spend it only on the few domains that matter (calendar, work, email), deliberately letting low-stakes areas (pantry, car) stay messy. 'Be organized everywhere' is doomed; 'be organized where you need to be' is achievable.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • The blank face isn't an empty mind

    A vacant, 'gormless' or zombie-like face during ADHD daydreaming reads as laziness but is the opposite — an OVERABUNDANCE of internal activity. The overthinking can burn a week's worth of energy by mid-morning, so looking like you did nothing can mean you're genuinely exhausted.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Voice the thoughts that hit the brake

    Close out the anxious self-monitoring loops out loud ('do I smell / look good / is he enjoying it') — by talking about them and getting reassurance, rather than letting them stop the orgasm.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Stop fighting your brain — understand it instead

    The shift is from forcing your brain into ill-fitting environments, relationships and self-versions to observing and accommodating how it actually works. The difficulty is usually a mismatch between the brain and its surroundings, not a defect in the brain — so the move is self-understanding, not self-suppression.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Your plate is different (don't compare, don't trust the TikTok checklist)

    Neurodivergence is a 'buffet of diversity': everyone loads a different set of traits (ADHD, autism, dyslexia, aphantasia), so your presentation, treatment and coping will differ. So (1) don't compare your plate to someone else's — 'they've got it together, why don't I' is unfair; (2) don't believe 'if you don't have these 5 traits you're not ADHD' — every diagnosis is as unique as a fingerprint.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Use mental models as decision filters

    Pre-built mental models and decision procedures act as external filters that interrupt impulsive decisions and reduce spiralling — instead of trying to suppress an impulse with willpower, give the brain a concrete process to follow, so the pause becomes structural rather than effortful.

    D · none / theory 1 source

Why this happens

Usually several mechanisms stack at once. Click to understand which one is yours.

  • The cost of masking

    Masking from a young age can be a form of self-harm — pretending to be someone else has a cost (exhaustion, anxiety, not knowing yourself) and makes it harder to recognise your own neurodivergence.

  • Alexithymia (trouble recognising and naming emotion)

    You're emotionally driven yet can't recognise or name the emotion. Combined with RSD and impaired interoception it forms a vicious loop: you feel a lot, don't know what, and the body goes into shutdown before you can intervene.

  • Oppositional defiance (turned outward, then inward)

    A reflex to reject what an authority says because of WHO said it, not whether it's true — so even correct, helpful advice gets thrown out. Learned against others, it gets internalized and turns on your own goals as self-sabotage.

  • Person-environment mismatch (not a defective brain)

    Much neurodivergent difficulty comes from being placed in environments, demands and self-images built for a different kind of brain — a fit problem, not a broken-person problem. The same wiring that fails in a hostile setting can work well in a matched one, so the lever is the surroundings, not more willpower.
