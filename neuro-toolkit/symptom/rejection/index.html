Symptom

I can't handle rejection — rejection sensitivity (RSD)

Rejection and criticism hit you disproportionately hard, and you constantly feel like people are mad at you.

RSD is an intense pain around rejection — real, though not an official criterion. Below — how to not react on autopilot (the 24h rule), and ask for specifics instead of assuming the worst.

This page isn't typically flagged for the selected profile — shown because you opened it directly.

Methods that help

  • After sex, stay — and don't decline with a bare 'no' (RSD)

    For RSD: after sex don't abruptly get up and leave, and don't decline an initiation with a bare 'no' — explain why. An ADHD person experiences sudden rejection/abandonment far more intensely, which escalates to rows and breakups.

    B · good 1 source

  • It's not me, it's the monkey brain (three-brains technique)

    When RSD, shame, or an unhealthy impulse hits, attribute the feeling to 'little you' or your 'monkey brain', not to yourself: 'that's not me, that's my monkey brain wanting the snack / feeling rejected'. It's a cognitive trick that separates the reactive part from your adult self so you can treat it with compassion. Key: keep it SIMPLE.

    B · good 1 source

  • The 24-hour rule for an RSD hit

    There's no cure for an RSD hit, but space works: name it ('this is RSD'), say nothing in the moment, and defer the reaction — if it still matters in a day, then raise it.

    C · weak / preliminary 8 sources

  • Ask for the praise first (feedback sandwich)

    To make criticism bearable with RSD, ask explicitly: 'first tell me what you love, let me dwell in it, then the notes' — or sandwich the critique between two praises.

    C · weak / preliminary 4 sources

  • Defend your diagnosis against the stereotype

    Expect pushback — 'you make eye contact, you talk well, you have empathy, you can't be autistic' — proportional to how far you diverge from the person's mental image of autism (a movie, one documentary). Don't argue trait-by-trait. Weigh their lay impression against the hours of structured interviews and questionnaires done by a trained clinician, and treat their disbelief as their own learning curve, separate from your identity.

    C · weak / preliminary 3 sources

  • Observer mode (view yourself from a bird's eye)

    Instead of being flooded by emotion (e.g. RSD), deliberately step away from yourself and observe yourself as an outsider. It's a skill, not a trait — and it's trained mainly through the body (EMDR, hypnotherapy, breath), not talk alone.

    C · weak / preliminary 3 sources

  • Detective technique: hunt the catalyst and test RSD from the outside

    On a blow-up, hunt for 'the seagull that pushed you off the cliff' — what happened EARLIER, not the last visible thing. And on RSD catastrophizing ('I'll get fired') run the third-person test: 'if a colleague sent that email, would they get fired?'.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Open up by gradualization

    Don't lead with the label or a big disclosure. Start with a universally relatable topic (isolation, working from home), test the water in small graded steps, and read whether you're met with appreciation before going deeper — a door-wedge that protects against rejection and lets you build up to the bigger things.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • A four-step plan for a hard disclosure

    Turn coming out as autistic into a small, deliberate plan: write a one-or-two-sentence script leading with the most important line, pick a moment with positive energy and space to absorb it, prepare for the realistic response (not the best case), and then just do it. Say your few phrases and stop — wait for their response instead of over-explaining.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • A weekly RSD risk check-in

    RSD's real damage is the protective walls — people-pleasing, not taking risks — built to avoid being triggered. Counter it with a weekly self check-in: 'Have I faced RSD at least once this week?', meaning did I take a risk I could have been rejected for. If not, go find one.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • An exit plan and your own ride home

    Decide before you arrive exactly when and how you'll get home, and keep control of your own departure (drive yourself, even separately from a partner). The certainty alone tames anxiety so you can actually be present — and the 'Irish goodbye' plus a follow-up text short-circuits the rejection spiral.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Ask for the details before you spiral

    When someone says 'let's talk later', ask for the details now ('I know I'll overthink this — just give me clarity'), because your imagined version can be 50x worse than reality.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Bank reassurance in advance against RSD

    Ask the people close to you to tell you often — proactively — that they love and care about you, and offer the same back. When you snap from overwhelm and RSD tells you you're unloved and a horrible person, that recently-banked reassurance is concrete counter-evidence that a snap was just overstimulation, not proof.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Check the comparison, not just the criticism

    Ego is anything that compares you to another person — and flattery that ranks you above someone else is ego too, a hidden source of suffering despite feeling good. Treat comparative praise with the same caution as criticism, and let awareness step in after an automatic reaction to defuse it.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Delay every invitation, then default to no

    Never answer an invitation or time-demand in the moment. Say you'll check your diary and reply within a couple of days, hide your phone, and use the window to run a written pros-and-cons list. Default to declining or delaying rather than accepting — it's far easier to upgrade a no to a yes than to downgrade a yes to a no.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Make boundaries specific and keep them visible

    An easily-excitable ADHD brain forgets vague boundaries the moment a tempting offer arrives, and 'do less' has no defined limit so you drift back to doing just as much. Make boundaries specific and measurable ('two talks a month', not 'less'), and keep them physically visible so an exciting email can't quietly override them.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Name your RSD as a character to disarm it

    Give your rejection-sensitivity a separate, cartoonish name — 'Dave the Dragon' — so you can externalise it. When it flares, recognise it as that creature reacting to thousands of old wounds rather than to what actually just happened: the pain is very real, but it's not based in present reality.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Name your inner critic as a character

    Externalize the inner critic as a named character (e.g. 'Critical Kelvin'), recognize it formed as a protective coping mechanism, then thank it and gently disagree — 'I know you sound like a jerk, but you're trying to keep me safe; thank you, but I think you're wrong'. Treat it with kindness instead of fighting or silencing it.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Outsource logistics and boundary-enforcement to someone else

    ADHD means you won't remember to ask for the details until it's too late, and an excitable people-pleasing brain caves on boundaries 'just this once'. Hand both jobs to a third party: have someone gather event logistics on your behalf via an automated reminder, and let them decline offers that break your rules — they aren't subject to your in-the-moment impulses.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Play the numbers to blunt rejection

    Over-focusing on one thing you might be rejected for amplifies RSD. Spread the risk instead: apply broadly, expect roughly a one-in-ten hit rate, and plan for rejection as the default. When any single 'no' is expected statistical noise rather than a personal verdict, it stops mattering so much.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Reach the root, not the band-aid

    Don't stop at 'I shouldn't take it to heart' — that's a band-aid. Ask the deeper question: why does THIS resonate with me when I've let other things go? Tracing why a specific jab lands reaches the root insecurity instead of patching the reaction.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Reframe mistreatment as a difference in perspective

    Facing intolerance, disrespect or someone's hurtful behaviour, deliberately consider that they are seeing the situation from a completely different perspective — 'we each see the sun from our own point of view'. This is not excusing them; it's a piece of cognitive distance that reduces the personal sting and makes the experience more bearable to sit through.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Reframe an RSD spiral with a four-step evidence check

    An RSD spiral can't be argued out of the feeling, but a short structured step buys a breather to climb out. Name the thought you're believing, list what happens if you believe it, state the opposite thought and its consequences, then weigh the actual evidence for each — usually thin for the negative, ample for the positive.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Send the short honest text before avoidance compounds

    Leaving a party without goodbyes or forgetting to reply feels too awkward to fix — and the awkwardness only grows the longer you avoid it, until a day becomes weeks and a friendship quietly ends. Bite the bullet and send the short honest message immediately: 'sorry I had to go, wasn't feeling great, lovely night.' Reversed, 99% of people react with 'no worries.'

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source
