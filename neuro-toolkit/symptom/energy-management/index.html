Symptom

I run out of energy long before the day is done

Your daily energy is a limited budget — spoons — and pushing past it triggers the crash. Below: pace the day, schedule recharge, and protect capacity before it's gone.

Energy management is the pacing layer that sits between sleep and burnout: not the night’s rest, not the eventual crash, but the daily budget you spend down. Below: ways to read your own gauge, dose demands, and bank recovery before you’re empty.

This page isn't typically flagged for the selected profile — shown because you opened it directly.

Methods that help

  • A movement break when focus fades

    Concentration wanes after a fixed window (~20 minutes). A short physical break — shoot some hoops, a quick walk — resets attention and prevents total disengagement. As one teacher put it: lose them for 10 minutes, or lose them for the whole day, week, or career.

    C · weak / preliminary 3 sources

  • Movement snacks for a dopamine and focus bump

    Tiny bursts of movement give dopamine a regulating bump and even sharpen focus. Do ~20 squats while brushing your teeth or a few before a high-stakes task; balance on one leg while the kettle boils — single-leg balance recruits the cerebellum, which boosts the prefrontal cortex.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Pace your days by neurotype

    The AuDHD hyperactive side and the autistic side that needs calm and routine pull against each other. Rather than run both flat out, label an out-and-about day a 'hyperactive day' and deliberately book a quiet, low-demand 'autism-friendly' recovery day after it — routine days bank the energy that a spontaneous day spends.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Protein and fat first (build a blood-sugar dam)

    Build a breakfast and meals around protein and fat, and eat the fat/protein/fibre BEFORE the sugar, carb or coffee. Protein supplies the building blocks for dopamine and serotonin; eating it first acts like a dam that slows sugar absorption, blunting the spike-and-crash that wrecks focus.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Use a fixed daily routine as pre-solved time management

    For an autistic brain that finds time estimation effortful and anxiety-provoking, a repeated daily routine does the time management for you. When wake, drop-off, coffee and commute happen at the same times every day, there are no calculations left to make — the 'how long will this take' load disappears, freeing capacity for genuinely new events.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • A non-negotiable daily baseline (movement, breakfast, sleep)

    Stabilise the brain's chemistry with a fixed daily baseline you don't debate: intense movement early, the same high-protein high-fat breakfast every morning, and a fixed sleep window everything else adjusts around — plus a single structure you follow without deciding, so executive function isn't spent on the basics.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Buy frozen pre-chopped food to beat forgetting

    Fresh produce depends on remembering to use it before it spoils — a bet ADHD short-term memory keeps losing, turning food into mould and wasted money. Swap to frozen pre-chopped vegetables: they don't go off if forgotten, and the prep is already done, so a memory lapse costs nothing.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Contain hyperfocus with an external stop and floors

    Don't try to reduce fixation — bound it. Pre-define a hard stopping rule for each focus session (one defined unit plus a hard cap) and pair it with daily non-negotiable floors for the basics, so the productive hyperfocus is contained rather than allowed to cannibalize sleep, food and movement.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Creatine for the brain, not just muscle

    Creatine — familiar as a strength supplement — also supports brain energy metabolism, with reported benefits for memory, mood and cognition, including when you're sleep deprived. For neurodivergent people with disrupted sleep, it's a low-cost lever worth considering, with usual caveats about checking supplements with a professional.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Delay every invitation, then default to no

    Never answer an invitation or time-demand in the moment. Say you'll check your diary and reply within a couple of days, hide your phone, and use the window to run a written pros-and-cons list. Default to declining or delaying rather than accepting — it's far easier to upgrade a no to a yes than to downgrade a yes to a no.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Keep the first 90 minutes of the day slow

    The first inputs after waking calibrate the nervous system's model for how the rest of the day is anticipated to go. A slow, low-stimulation, ideally social start sets a calmer baseline than a high-stimulation rush into the phone.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Leave at orange, not red

    Leaving an event itself costs energy — saying goodbyes, navigating, getting stopped on the way out. So leave while you still have reserve ('orange'), not once you're already drained ('red'). If you can't sense the threshold, use a concrete external cue instead.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Map your burnout indicators

    High-energy neurodivergent living can bring recurring burnout (for some, roughly quarterly). It has personal warning indicators — stuttering, making up words, anxiety needing breathwork, anger turning to tears — so learn yours and ease off before the crash instead of measuring success by never crashing.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Set yourself up the way you'd set up a kid for a party

    Before a draining event, get fed, rested and hydrated — exactly what you'd ensure for a child before a party. Carry snacks and water and 'pre-game' with those instead of alcohol. Meeting basic physiological needs raises your baseline regulation, so you have more tolerance for the sensory and social load.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Pick tasks by energy, not by importance

    Check your current energy first, then assign tasks that fit it — matching demand to your fluctuating energy state prevents the mismatch that triggers avoidance. Drop 'eat the frog' as a default: leading with the most aversive task front-loads emotional friction and can stall the whole day.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Plan a social event like you'd plan a work talk

    Treat a social event as a planned event you take seriously: research who'll be there, prep a few light topics, sleep and eat beforehand. Offloading the work to BEFORE the event removes the in-the-moment cognitive load a neurodivergent brain can't reliably improvise.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Remove a daily decision by wearing the same outfit

    Choosing what to wear every morning quietly burns executive-function bandwidth. Find one outfit you like the feel of, buy several copies, and wear it daily — eliminating the decision erases a large hidden chunk of morning friction and frees mental capacity for real work.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Schedule self-care inside work hours, not as leftover free time

    Recovery time squeezed into 'free time' off the clock never materializes against an endless task pile. Block it on the work calendar as a job duty, reduce total work to fit it, and invert the 'no time' logic: the busier you are, the more recovery you need.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • The blank face isn't an empty mind

    A vacant, 'gormless' or zombie-like face during ADHD daydreaming reads as laziness but is the opposite — an OVERABUNDANCE of internal activity. The overthinking can burn a week's worth of energy by mid-morning, so looking like you did nothing can mean you're genuinely exhausted.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Track your omega-3 index once a year

    Omega-3 insufficiency is extremely common, and a blood test makes supplementation objective instead of a guess. Measure your omega-3 index annually and aim for a value above 8% (a 9–11% target), so you can confirm your intake is actually moving the number rather than assuming it is.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Train the brain with a fast racket sport

    Fast, strategic racket sports load several brain regions at once — the cerebellum (coordination and processing speed), the parietal lobes (tracking the ball in space) and the frontal lobes (constant strategy). Table tennis is a strong pick because it's fast and strategic; get a coach and actually get good, since the skill-building is what does the work.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Train the shut-off, not just the stress

    Burnout is rarely a coping problem — it's an input problem plus a lost ability to come back down. Audit where you're leaking bandwidth, cut low-ROI inputs, and train the return from stress to rest with cardio, brief deliberate stressors, and direct parasympathetic activation.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Cut aspartame and watch what changes

    Zero-calorie diet drinks aren't automatically weight-neutral or harmless — aspartame has been associated with obesity and diabetes (proposed via gut-microbiome and metabolic effects), and some people find joint pain resolves when they stop it and returns when they restart. Run a personal elimination-and-watch test, expect quitting to feel habit-forming, and consider stevia as a swap.

    D · none / theory 1 source

Why this happens

Usually several mechanisms stack at once. Click to understand which one is yours.

  • Allostatic load: the stress that never switched off

    Burnout isn't one big event — it's the cumulative wear of a nervous system that keeps stealing resources from tomorrow to meet today, and never fully shuts back down.

  • The blood-sugar / dopamine spike and crash

    A sugary, refined-carb hit spikes blood sugar and, alongside it, dopamine — which feels great briefly, but the steep drop that follows takes both down below baseline, leaving lethargy, poor focus and a renewed craving to re-spike.

  • Hyperfocus tunneling (the drift that drops everything else)

    The same locked-in attention that makes hyperfocus productive narrows awareness so much that competing needs — hunger, fatigue, time, movement — drop out of perception, so basic self-maintenance silently collapses while the focus holds.
