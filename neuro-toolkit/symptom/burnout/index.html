Energy and burnout

Your head signs you up for more than the body can carry; even beloved work in hyperfocus can end in burnout.

Energy management is the foundation of ADHD life — more than ‘more discipline’. Below: an energy audit (must-vs-want, a parking lot, ‘tomorrow is a new game’), active rest, and saying out loud how your brain works.

This page isn't typically flagged for the selected profile — shown because you opened it directly.

Methods that help

  • A 20-minute run for the dopamine hit

    Movement acts like a dose of dopamine that quietens the mind and brings you into the present — but the big release comes after ~20 minutes, so a 20-30 min run (even very slow) gives ~80% of the mental benefit.

    B · good 3 sources

  • Active rest (movement that engages the mind)

    Passive rest doesn't work in ADHD. Rest is 'doing the opposite of what tires you' — ideally movement that DEMANDS full attention and switches thinking off.

    B · good 3 sources

  • Five brain pillars (sleep, stress, hormones, gut, movement)

    Five physiological levers that make ADHD better or worse and can be optimised even before/without a diagnosis: sleep, stress, hormones, gut health, and movement. Manage them well and you likely need less medication.

    B · good 3 sources

  • Mindfulness in movement (and 20 star jumps on a bad day)

    The two best-evidenced ADHD interventions are mindfulness and exercise. Still mindfulness is very hard with ADHD — do it in active form (mindful running, dancing, music). Even 20 star jumps on a bad-weather day shifts the whole day's trajectory.

    B · good 1 source

  • Energy audit: must-vs-want, parking lot, fresh day

    Ask whether the BODY can carry what the HEAD signed up for. Keep a 'parking lot' for what you consciously won't do, and start each day from zero.

    C · weak / preliminary 6 sources

  • Name overstimulation and plan your exit

    When you feel overstimulated, say it aloud ('I'm overstimulated, I'm stepping out for 10 minutes') — and learn to spot the CIRCUMSTANCES of risk, to plan your exit in advance.

    C · weak / preliminary 5 sources

  • Tell people how your brain works

    Say it plainly: 'this'll take me 13× more energy than it would Kasia — let's find another way'. It's an energy strategy, not an excuse.

    C · weak / preliminary 5 sources

  • HALT and trigger stacking

    On an outsize reaction, don't assume 'the last thing' is to blame. Count the stacked small stressors (trigger stacking) and check the basics with HALT: Hungry, Angry, Lonely, Tired.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Judge control by whether you can stop

    Hyper-focusing on productive work can be as out-of-control as a gaming addiction — extreme output isn't proof of discipline, it can be compulsion on autopilot. Judge whether you're in control by whether you can STOP, not by how many hours you produce.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Build an internal source of calm, not event-based highs

    Happiness tied to external events is inconsistent — you can't reproduce the same good thing every day, so relying on it stays unstable. Durable contentment has to be cultivated as an internal, self-generated baseline; recognize each external high as a non-renewable source and deliberately build inner calm instead.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Check the fulfilment, not just the metrics

    Repeatedly doubling down on whatever others react to turns you into a 'caricature of yourself' and drives burnout — you end up making things you aren't fulfilled by. And you can't satisfy everyone's contradictory feedback anyway, so check whether the work still fulfills you, not just whether it performs.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Contain hyperfocus with an external stop and floors

    Don't try to reduce fixation — bound it. Pre-define a hard stopping rule for each focus session (one defined unit plus a hard cap) and pair it with daily non-negotiable floors for the basics, so the productive hyperfocus is contained rather than allowed to cannibalize sleep, food and movement.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Design for your worst week, with a return path built in

    Build your systems around your failures, not your successes: a routine that only works on a good week collapses on a bad one. Design for the low-capacity week first, and bake in a restart protocol so a single missed day doesn't snowball into months of abandonment.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Hunt for the AND, not the either/or

    A mind under pressure defaults to 'me OR my goal', which forces self-sacrifice. Catch the binary, refuse the premise, and deliberately look for the AND — start later, cap the hours, set limits — so you take care of yourself AND keep doing the work. And you don't have to do it all alone.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Keep the first 90 minutes of the day slow

    The first inputs after waking calibrate the nervous system's model for how the rest of the day is anticipated to go. A slow, low-stimulation, ideally social start sets a calmer baseline than a high-stimulation rush into the phone.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Map your burnout indicators

    High-energy neurodivergent living can bring recurring burnout (for some, roughly quarterly). It has personal warning indicators — stuttering, making up words, anxiety needing breathwork, anger turning to tears — so learn yours and ease off before the crash instead of measuring success by never crashing.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Map your peak window (schedule hard tasks to your chronotype)

    Instead of 'wake up earlier', map when across the 24h you're genuinely at your best (ADHD often has a delayed phase / night-owl pattern) and put your most important tasks in that window — even if it's counterintuitive. Not sure when? Track your energy for a few days or ask a loved one.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Move through stress with intention, not the old pattern

    Resilience isn't less stress — it's the bandwidth to move through the same stress with intention instead of defaulting to old survival patterns. The catch: the nervous system prefers a familiar hell over an unfamiliar heaven, so the new pattern has to be practised until it can win.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Practice, not discipline — and treat self-care as an experiment

    You'll never become 'disciplined' with an ADHD brain, so stop fighting impulse with willpower. Build habits by practice (each attempt is a rep, expect to forget), and do a recovery activity as an experiment to OBSERVE your guilt rather than to 'fix' yourself.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Ride the energy wave (catch the window, don't force the start)

    When even breaking the task down doesn't help, the bottleneck is initiation, not size. Don't force the start with willpower: on no-energy days let it go ('today's not the day'), but the moment energy shows up — act IMMEDIATELY, don't delay and don't wait for 'more' to come.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Schedule self-care inside work hours, not as leftover free time

    Recovery time squeezed into 'free time' off the clock never materializes against an endless task pile. Block it on the work calendar as a job duty, reduce total work to fit it, and invert the 'no time' logic: the busier you are, the more recovery you need.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Shutdown / reboot — give space

    After intense activity a neurodivergent person may need full silence and space ('shutdown / reboot'). The key message for a partner: going quiet and unresponsive isn't anger or 'it's about you', it's a need to recharge — agree on it in advance.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Solve overwhelm by subtracting, not adding

    When overwhelmed, the instinct is to ADD a fix — a tool, a habit, more 'self-care'. But true rest needs you to actively REMOVE things from your baseline, because so much daily activity runs on autopilot that 'doing nothing' just preserves the load. Before adding anything, ask what you could take away.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Train the shut-off, not just the stress

    Burnout is rarely a coping problem — it's an input problem plus a lost ability to come back down. Audit where you're leaking bandwidth, cut low-ROI inputs, and train the return from stress to rest with cardio, brief deliberate stressors, and direct parasympathetic activation.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • The enjoyment test: laziness or overwhelm

    What looks like laziness is usually overwhelm. The test: laziness would be a choice and would feel good; overwhelm is lying there with NO enjoyment while a merciless inner critic tears you up. And the shame of 'only I can't cope' disarms in daylight — in a group of other ADHD adults.

    D · none / theory 4 sources

Why this happens

Usually several mechanisms stack at once. Click to understand which one is yours.

  • The cost of masking

    Masking from a young age can be a form of self-harm — pretending to be someone else has a cost (exhaustion, anxiety, not knowing yourself) and makes it harder to recognise your own neurodivergence.

  • ADHD ↔ autism opposing pull

    In someone with both ADHD and autism the two profiles can pull in opposite directions: ADHD drives impulsive action (novelty, dopamine from the thought), and the autism rejects it moments after starting (effort, stimuli, overwhelm) — hence a 'start-then-abort' cycle.

  • Impaired interoception (delayed body signals)

    In some neurodivergent people the brain reads internal body signals weakly or late — hunger, fullness, thirst, fatigue, needing the toilet. The research is mixed, but where it applies the signal can be too faint or too late.

  • Tidal dopamine and noradrenaline (not low, dysregulated)

    In ADHD dopamine and noradrenaline aren't simply low — they're dysregulated like a tide: sometimes out (scattered, unfocused, flat), sometimes rushing in (hyperfocus, hyper-energy). How you are in half an hour can be totally different.

  • The brain fixes problems by adding, not subtracting

    Faced with a problem, people overwhelmingly reach for something to ADD — a new tool, habit, app, commitment — and rarely consider removing something, even when subtraction is the simpler fix. This default quietly piles load onto an already-overloaded system.

  • Allostatic load: the stress that never switched off

    Burnout isn't one big event — it's the cumulative wear of a nervous system that keeps stealing resources from tomorrow to meet today, and never fully shuts back down.

  • Avoidance as the real addiction

    The modern compulsion isn't to any one game, feed or substance — it's to avoidance itself. Burnout's core is not overwork but not knowing how to rest, so each 'fun' hit just teaches you to escape the inner state you won't face.

  • Familiarity mistaken for safety

    A dysregulated nervous system defaults to what it knows and codes the familiar as 'safe' — even when the familiar is chaos. It will choose a familiar hell over an unfamiliar heaven.

  • Hyperfocus tunneling (the drift that drops everything else)

    The same locked-in attention that makes hyperfocus productive narrows awareness so much that competing needs — hunger, fatigue, time, movement — drop out of perception, so basic self-maintenance silently collapses while the focus holds.
