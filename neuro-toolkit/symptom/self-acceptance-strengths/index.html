Symptom

I want to stop fighting myself and like who I am

Not every day is a crisis — sometimes the work is accepting your brain and using it as a strength. Below: reframing neurodivergence as difference-not-defect, and building real self-acceptance.

The deficit story isn’t the only one. Below: ways to separate the trait from the shame, recognise where your wiring is genuinely an asset, and build an identity that isn’t built on fixing yourself.

This page isn't typically flagged for the selected profile — shown because you opened it directly.

Methods that help

  • Defend your diagnosis against the stereotype

    Expect pushback — 'you make eye contact, you talk well, you have empathy, you can't be autistic' — proportional to how far you diverge from the person's mental image of autism (a movie, one documentary). Don't argue trait-by-trait. Weigh their lay impression against the hours of structured interviews and questionnaires done by a trained clinician, and treat their disbelief as their own learning curve, separate from your identity.

    C · weak / preliminary 3 sources

  • Catch and challenge internalized ableism

    After a diagnosis, one of the hardest struggles is the voice you turn on yourself — 'pull yourself together', 'just be normal', 'ignore this'. That's internalized ableism: years of an ableist 'don't be different' absorbed into your self-talk, and it suppresses genuine needs. Notice it, name it as internalized ableism to unlearn rather than valid instruction, and stop treating self-understanding as if it should automatically fix everything.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Check resonance with another autistic person

    The strongest self-recognition signal isn't a behaviour checklist — it's finding someone who knows they're autistic, ideally similar in age, gender and culture, and noticing whether their hidden internal struggles resonate with you to a degree you didn't think possible. Shared internal experience tracks common neurology far better than visible behaviour.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • The interest test: dopamine hit vs grounding

    To tell an ADHD interest from an autistic special interest, ask why it's pursued and for how long. ADHD interest is a short-lived dopamine hit that fades once the novelty is gone; an autistic special interest is returned to over a long time for comfort, harmony and grounding — and it doubles as a self-regulation tool.

    C · weak / preliminary 2 sources

  • Find a teacher who adapts to your body

    When a physical trait blocks the standard technique — hypermobile fingers that won't stay straight and keep flicking off an instrument — the fix isn't to force 'the proper way' and beat yourself up. Seek an open-minded teacher whose instinct is 'let's find another finger, let's find another way'. Adapting the method to your body replaces the shame spiral with joy, and the same principle generalises well beyond music.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Handle a blind spot with curiosity, not defense

    By definition you can't see your own blind spots, so trusted people (often a partner or kids) are your only window. When someone flags a behavior, run three steps: find it, get curious about it instead of defensive, then be okay with it — approach with curiosity, not judgment.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Hold the strengths and the struggle together

    The hard parts of neurodivergence and its real strengths — pattern recognition, creative problem-solving, hyperfocus, the spark — are both true at the same time, and neither cancels the other. The reframe is to keep the whole picture rather than collapsing into a pure-deficit or pure-superpower story.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Normalize neurodivergence in everyday talk

    You can build acceptance and quietly plant seeds of self-recognition without ever telling anyone they might be autistic — just talk about your own neurodivergence casually, as one everyday topic among others ('I like tea, I like coffee, and I'm neurodivergent'). Repeated low-key normalizing lowers stigma and helps others recognise themselves, especially those who can't access a formal diagnosis.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Point the energy, don't suppress it

    ADHD energy is a permanent baseline you ARE, not an illness that comes on — which makes it hard to recognize, since you've never been anyone else. It's like storm-wind on a sailing boat: the same force can capsize you or speed you forward. Self-knowledge lets you angle the sails and steer it constructively.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Read the need for sameness as anxiety, not control

    An autistic person's insistence on sameness looks controlling or even manipulative, but it isn't about controlling other people — it's an anxiety response about their own experience, and the visible meltdown at a change is usually the top of a build-up over days, weeks or months, not a reaction to the one small change in front of you. Seeing it this way changes how you respond, to yourself or to someone you support.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Recognise pebbling and acts of service as love languages

    Autistic care often shows up not as verbal comfort but as pebbling — giving small objects or, especially, pieces of information ('I saw this and thought you'd like it') — and as acts of service: bringing food, offering to walk the dog. Neurotypicals can misread these as not caring; recognise them as sincere expressions of love.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Redirect a harmful stim instead of stopping it

    Stims aren't all distress signals — some aid concentration, some discharge anxiety, some express joy. When a stim becomes harmful (skin-picking under stress, to the point of bleeding), don't try to suppress it; pre-decide a less-harmful outlet for when the urge hits — playing with hair, sensory jewellery or 'chewellery', a fidget toy.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Revisit childhood activities to reconnect

    Sensory cues — smell, music, taste — can suddenly unlock long-forgotten memories. When your brain feels stuck or you've lost yourself to years of masking, deliberately redo childhood activities (drawing, swimming, old foods) and lean into the senses to surface what feels locked away and reconnect with who you are.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Treat a diagnostic report as a snapshot, not a forecast

    A diagnostic assessment captures one moment's presentation and lists concerns and predicted deficits — it is not a prognosis. Those predictions often prove wrong (the report worried you'd never make friends; then came six birthday invitations in three months). Read the report for insight, hold its projections loosely, and remember you retain agency to develop far beyond what it foresaw.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Stop fighting your brain — understand it instead

    The shift is from forcing your brain into ill-fitting environments, relationships and self-versions to observing and accommodating how it actually works. The difficulty is usually a mismatch between the brain and its surroundings, not a defect in the brain — so the move is self-understanding, not self-suppression.

    C · weak / preliminary 1 source

  • Person-environment mismatch (not a defective brain)

    Much neurodivergent difficulty comes from being placed in environments, demands and self-images built for a different kind of brain — a fit problem, not a broken-person problem. The same wiring that fails in a hostile setting can work well in a matched one, so the lever is the surroundings, not more willpower.
