Method

Evidence & wins journal (against imposter syndrome)

After each feared event, journal the evidence it went well; also keep a daily wins journal — the ADHD brain forgets successes fast, so confidence never accumulates.

This page isn't typically flagged for the selected profile — shown because you opened it directly.

Imposter syndrome feeds on the absence of remembered evidence: fear erases the memory that last time went fine. The countermeasure is mechanical — after each feared event (a talk, a conversation, a performance) deliberately and immediately write down that it went well: what people said afterwards, that they came up and praised you. Build a journal of counter-evidence you return to before the next time.

The point is to have concrete, stored material next time to counter ‘it definitely won’t go well’ — instead of relying on a memory that, under fear, drops the positives.

What the research says

Scientific grade verified against the literature. No entries = no direct studies (graded from mechanism/experience).

  • A systematic review and meta-analysis of CBT interventions based on the Fennell model of low self-esteem
    meta-analysis · 2018

    Meta-analysis of 8 CBT-for-low-self-esteem studies (7 in quantitative synthesis) using Fennell's model, which centrally employs a 'positive data log' (recording evidence/positive events that contradict negative core beliefs). Large effect on self-esteem for weekly-session interventions (d=1.12; 0.34 for one-day workshops). The evidence/wins journal IS this positive-data-log technique, but it is bundled within full CBT, not tested in isolation, and samples were not ADHD/autism-specific.
  • Cognitive behaviour therapy for low self-esteem: A preliminary randomized controlled trial in a primary care setting
    RCT · 2012

    RCT of Fennell-model CBT for low self-esteem in primary care showing significant improvement vs control; the protocol includes positive-data-logging as a component, but the journal alone was not isolated and the population was not ADHD/autism.
  • The effectiveness of online educational interventions on impostor syndrome and burnout among medical trainees: a systematic review
    review · 2024

    Systematic review: two RCTs found significant reductions in impostor-syndrome symptoms after structured interventions using cognitive reframing; reviews recommend keeping a record of accomplishments/positive feedback as an external reference point. Indirect support for the imposter-syndrome framing; not ADHD/autism samples and the journal is not tested as a standalone.
  • Thankful for the Little Things: A Meta-Analysis of Gratitude Interventions (Davis et al.)
    meta-analysis · 2016

    Meta-analysis of gratitude/positive-event journaling interventions shows small-to-moderate benefits to well-being and mood vs neutral controls. Adjacent technique (deliberately recording positive experiences) but a different target than self-worth/imposter feelings and not in ADHD/autism.

What the grade means

A A — strongest evidence: meta-analyses or RCTs directly confirm it works (or, for diagnostic tools, strong validation of accuracy).
B B — good evidence: a single RCT, or a strong mechanism with supporting studies.
C C — weak / preliminary: a plausible mechanism, but few direct, controlled tests.
D D — no evidence: theory or isolated anecdotes, no studies.
