Method

Speak about yourself kindly (and 'the old me')

Against chronic self-criticism: speak to yourself kindly (as you would to a friend) and frame old self-critical traits as 'that was the old me'. Caveat from the research: forced grand affirmations ('I'm wonderful') can backfire for people with low self-esteem — what works is kindness and realism, not positive slogans by force.

This page isn't typically flagged for the selected profile — shown because you opened it directly.

A neurodivergent brain receives tens of thousands more negative messages through childhood than a neurotypical one; these compound into a chronic, harsh inner monologue. The counterweight is NOT forcing praise on yourself — it’s self-compassion: speak to yourself the way you’d speak to a friend in trouble.

Important caveat from the research: repeated grand affirmations like ‘I’m wonderful / lovable’ can make people with low self-esteem feel worse (Wood 2009) — they clash with what you actually believe. So aim for kind but CREDIBLE statements (‘today was hard and I did what I could’), not declarations you don’t buy.

A separate, helpful move is temporal distancing: frame old self-critical traits in the past tense and as ‘a previous person’ — ‘I used to be like that, but I don’t want to be anymore’. This peels the labels off who you are now (not an affirmation, but reframing identity over time). Writing by hand can help, as it slows you down and consolidates.

What the research says

Scientific grade verified against the literature. No entries = no direct studies (graded from mechanism/experience).

  • A systematic review and meta-analysis of the effectiveness of self-compassion-related interventions
    meta-analysis · 2022

    Across ~20 RCTs (19 papers, 1,350 participants), self-compassion-related interventions produced a significant medium reduction in self-criticism vs. controls — supporting the kind-self-relating mechanism this method invokes.
  • Positive Self-Statements: Power for Some, Peril for Others (Wood, Perunovic & Lee)
    RCT · 2009

    In two experiments, people with LOW self-esteem who repeated a positive self-statement ('I'm a lovable person') felt WORSE than those who did not — the canonical evidence that 'repeat it until you believe it' backfires for exactly the population this method targets (people who feel broken).
  • The role of self-compassion in the mental health of adults with ADHD
    cohort study · 2022

    In adults with ADHD, higher self-compassion was associated with better mental health, suggesting self-compassion buffers ADHD-related distress — but this is correlational, not a test of repeated self-narrative rewriting.
  • Self-compassion as an antidote to self-stigma and shame in autistic adults (Riebel et al.)
    cohort study · 2025

    In autistic adults, self-compassion buffered the link between self-stigma/shame and poorer mental health; a related study found masking/camouflaging negatively correlates with self-compassion — relevant to the maskowanie-tozsamosc target but observational, not a trial of this technique.
  • Effectiveness of Self-Affirmation Interventions in Educational Settings: A Meta-Analysis
    meta-analysis · 2024

    Across 144 studies, self-affirmation showed only a low-magnitude average effect (d≈0.41), stronger under high threat, with effects moderated by individual characteristics — affirmation-style techniques work modestly and conditionally, not robustly.

What the grade means

A A — strongest evidence: meta-analyses or RCTs directly confirm it works (or, for diagnostic tools, strong validation of accuracy).
B B — good evidence: a single RCT, or a strong mechanism with supporting studies.
C C — weak / preliminary: a plausible mechanism, but few direct, controlled tests.
D D — no evidence: theory or isolated anecdotes, no studies.
